## 2. Full monodromy in degrees three and four

Work over the rational function field `C(T)`, where `T` is the varying target
coordinate.

### 2.1 The cubic

The trace-normalised nonpower cubic is

\[
q_T(X)=X^3+aX+T,\qquad a\ne0.
\]

Its discriminant is

\[
\Delta(T)=-4a^3-27T^2.
\]

Because `a != 0`, this polynomial has two distinct simple zeros.  Its divisor
in `C(T)` therefore has odd valuation at those zeros, so `Delta(T)` is not a
square in `C(T)`.  The monodromy is transitive and contains the three-cycle
given by inertia at infinity.  The only possibilities are consequently
`A_3` and `S_3`, and the nonsquare discriminant excludes `A_3`.  Thus

\[
G(q_T)=S_3.
\tag{2.1}
\]

### 2.2 The quartic

The trace-normalised quartic is

\[
q_T(X)=X^4+aX^2+cX+T,\qquad c\ne0.
\]

It is indecomposable.  Indeed, a quadratic--quadratic decomposition can be
written, after scaling leading coefficients, as

\[
q_T=g(h(X)),\qquad
h(X)=dX^2+pX+s.
\]

The coefficient of `X^3` in `g(h(X))` is a nonzero multiple of `p`.
Trace normalisation therefore forces `p=0`; but then `g(h(X))` is even and has
zero `X` coefficient, contradicting `c != 0`.

Polynomial indecomposability makes the monodromy primitive.  Inertia at
infinity supplies a four-cycle.  A primitive subgroup of `S_4` containing a
four-cycle is `S_4`: the cyclic and dihedral alternatives preserve the
two-block partition, while `A_4` contains no odd four-cycle.  Hence

\[
G(q_T)=S_4.
\tag{2.2}
\]

This short argument is all that is required from the low-dimensional
monodromy classification at the first rank-increasing factor.

## 3. Removing every root-dependent scaling

Let `n` be three or four.  Let `y_1,...,y_n` be the branches of the first
rank-increasing factor, and choose radicals

\[
u_i^m=y_i-b.
\]

At rank `n-1`, the map from the free `n`-dimensional constant vector space on
the `u_i` to their span has a one-dimensional kernel.  Choose its nonzero
generator

\[
\lambda_1u_1+\cdots+\lambda_nu_n=0.
\tag{3.1}
\]

All `lambda_i` are nonzero.  The set of zero coordinates is preserved under
monodromy: continuation only permutes the roots and multiplies radicals by
nonzero roots of unity, while the relation line is unique.  Since `S_n` is
transitive, the zero set is either empty or all indices.  It cannot be all
indices because (3.1) is nonzero.

Let a monodromy lift project to `sigma in S_n`.  There are `m`-th roots of
unity `zeta_i` such that

\[
u_i\longmapsto \zeta_i u_{\sigma(i)},
\qquad \zeta_i^m=1.
\]

Continuing (3.1) and using uniqueness of the relation gives a nonzero scalar
`c_gamma` with

\[
\lambda_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}\zeta_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}
   =c_\gamma\lambda_j.
\]

After taking `m`-th powers,

\[
L_{\sigma^{-1}(j)}=c_\gamma^mL_j,
\qquad L_i:=\lambda_i^m.
\tag{3.2}
\]

Thus the line generated by `L=(L_1,...,L_n)` is invariant under the complete
natural permutation action of `S_n`.

No representation classification is needed.  Take a transposition `(ij)`
and choose a third index `k`.  Projective invariance gives a scalar `d` such
that the transposition sends `L` to `dL`.  Since the transposition fixes `k`,

\[
L_k=dL_k.
\]

But `L_k != 0`, so `d=1`, and the two exchanged coordinates satisfy
`L_i=L_j`.  Transpositions generate `S_n`; therefore

\[
\lambda_1^m=\cdots=\lambda_n^m=:L\ne0.
\tag{3.3}
\]

Choose `rho in C` with `rho^m=L^{-1}` and replace `lambda_i` by
`rho lambda_i`.  With `v_i=rho lambda_i u_i`, equation (3.1) becomes
`sum v_i=0` and

\[
v_i^m=y_i-b
\tag{3.4}
\]

for every `i`.  This proves the scaling lemma without the withdrawn
scalar-collapse identity.

## 4. The master coefficient formula

Let

\[
\prod_{i=1}^n(1-v_it)=B(t)+e t^n,
\]

where `e` is the varying last elementary symmetric function.  For
`p_r=sum_i v_i^r`, logarithmic differentiation gives

\[
\frac{\partial p_m}{\partial e}
=-m[t^{m-n}]\frac1{B(t)+e t^n}.
\tag{4.1}
\]

In the formal power-series ring,

\[
\frac1{B(t)+e t^n}
=\sum_{k\ge0}(-e)^k t^{nk}B(t)^{-k-1}.
\tag{4.2}
\]

Consequently, the coefficient of `e^k` in (4.1) is the nonzero scalar
`-m(-1)^k` times

\[
[t^{m-n-nk}]B(t)^{-k-1}.
\tag{4.3}
\]

Distinct `k` give distinct powers of `e`, so there can be no cancellation
between the witnesses below.

### 4.1 Cubic coefficient formula

For `B(t)=1+a t^2`, the negative-binomial theorem gives

\[
[t^r](1+a t^2)^{-k-1}
=
\begin{cases}
(-1)^j\binom{k+j}{j}a^j,&r=2j,\\
0,&r\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
\tag{4.4}
\]

The only residual values needed are

\[
C_{k,0}=1,\qquad
C_{k,2}=-(k+1)a,\qquad
C_{k,4}=\binom{k+2}{2}a^2.
\tag{4.5}
\]

They are nonzero when `a != 0`.

### 4.2 Quartic coefficient formula

For `B(t)=1+a t^2+c t^3`, expand first by total degree and then by the
binomial theorem:

\[
[t^r]B(t)^{-k-1}
=\sum_{2u+3v=r}
(-1)^{u+v}
\binom{k+u+v}{u+v}
\binom{u+v}{u}a^u c^v.
\tag{4.6}
\]

The selected residuals are

\[
\begin{aligned}
C_{k,0}&=1,\\
C_{k,2}&=-(k+1)a,\\
C_{k,3}&=-(k+1)c,\\
C_{k,5}&=2\binom{k+2}{2}ac,\\
C_{k,6}\big|_{a=0}&=\binom{k+2}{2}c^2,\\
C_{k,9}\big|_{a=0}&=-\binom{k+3}{3}c^3.
\end{aligned}
\tag{4.7}
\]

Every applicable expression is nonzero under the stated hypotheses.

## 5. Uniform cubic exclusion

Assume `m >= 5`.  Euclidean division constructs a nonzero residual witness:

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
m\bmod3&k&r\text{ in }m=3+3k+r\\ \hline
0&(m-3)/3&0\\
2&(m-5)/3&2\\
1&(m-7)/3&4.
\end{array}
\tag{5.1}
\]

All displayed `k` are nonnegative in their cases.  By (4.5), the selected
coefficient of `partial p_m/partial e` is nonzero.  Thus no `m >= 5` can make
`p_m` independent of `e`.

At the remaining small values, `m=3` has residual coefficient `C_{0,0}=1`
and is excluded.  The only possibilities after the first power sum are
therefore `m=2,4`.  At `m=4`,

\[
\frac{\partial E_2(4)}{\partial e}=8ae\ne0,
\]

so only

\[
\boxed{m=2}
\]

survives.

## 6. Uniform quartic exclusion

First suppose `a != 0`.  For every `m >= 4`, except `m=5`, use

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
m\bmod4&k&r\text{ in }m=4+4k+r\\ \hline
0&(m-4)/4&0\\
2&(m-6)/4&2\\
3&(m-7)/4&3\\
1&(m-9)/4&5.
\end{array}
\tag{6.1}
\]

The residue-one line starts at `m=9`; its sole smaller value is the boundary
`m=5`.  Formula (4.7) excludes every selected case.

Now set `a=0`.  Excluding the temporary boundaries `m=5,6,9`, use

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
m\bmod4&k&r\\ \hline
0&(m-4)/4&0\\
3&(m-7)/4&3\\
2&(m-10)/4&6\\
1&(m-13)/4&9.
\end{array}
\tag{6.2}
\]

The last two rows start at `m=10` and `m=13`, respectively, explaining
exactly the exceptional small values.  Formula (4.7), with `c != 0`, again
gives a nonzero coefficient.

Including `m=2,3` below the quartic threshold, independence of the first power
sum is possible only at

\[
m=2,\quad m=3,\quad m=5,\quad
m=6\text{ with }a=0,\quad
m=9\text{ with }a=0.
\tag{6.3}
\]

The already verified second elementary-symmetric derivatives are

\[
\begin{array}{c|c}
m&\partial E_2(m)/\partial e\\ \hline
2&2,\\
3&-3a,\\
5&-5(a^3-2ae-c^2),\\
6\ (a=0)&6e^2,\\
9\ (a=0)&54c^2e^2.
\end{array}
\tag{6.4}
\]

For `m=5`, vanishing for every `e` first forces `a=0` from the coefficient of
`e`, and then `c=0` from the constant term, contradicting indecomposability.
The other rows are immediate.  Exactly one case remains:

\[
\boxed{m=3,\quad a=0}.
\]

It produces `Y(Y+c)^3+e^3` and the exceptional family `(x^4+cx)^3`.
